Course notes · Units 1–4 of 7

From a tiny computer to modern CPUs and GPUs

A course built as a chain of problems. Start with a machine that can run one instruction at a time from sixteen bytes of memory. Then, one lesson at a time, add exactly the mechanism needed to get past the machine's latest limitation, and say what it costs.

The goal: look at an unfamiliar CPU or GPU block diagram and explain why each major part exists, trace how a program uses it, and predict what limits performance. You need basic programming experience. No electronics, assembly or architecture background is assumed, and nothing has to be installed: every explorer runs in these pages.

Every lesson answers the same questions

Each lesson opens with a four-part card, so you always know why you're learning something before you learn how it works:

LimitationWhat can the current machine do, and what wall has it hit?
New mechanismWhat single new idea do we add to get past it?
New stateWhat does it have to remember, and who owns that state?
CostWhat does it cost in area, energy, complexity or predictability, and when does it fail to help?

Then comes an explanation with diagrams, a worked trace, an interactive explorer, Predict questions whose answers stay folded until you've committed to one, and a checkpoint to explain in your own words.

How to study with these notes

  • Treat lesson numbers as dependencies, not deadlines. Spend one session reading and a separate one predicting and experimenting. Pipelining hazards, out-of-order execution, and (later) memory ordering and GPU tiling deserve more than one pass.
  • Predict before you click. Every explorer is more useful if you've written down what you expect first. When you're wrong, that's the lesson.
  • Keep a one-paragraph notebook entry per lesson:

The old limitation was  . We added  . The new state is  . On the worked example,   changes. The cost is  . The idea fails to help when  . I can test that by  .

Move on when you can explain the mechanism without its diagram and solve a changed version of the example. If you can name the parts but can't trace the behaviour, do one more small trace before adding complexity.

Ground rules these notes follow

RuleWhy
Separate the instruction contract from any one implementationMost of this course is hidden machinery that must never change a program's results.
Label teaching machines, invented timings and ideal boundsNIB-8, the 5-stage model and the tiny cache are for tracing, not specifications of real chips.
Keep latency, throughput, bandwidth, capacity and energy apartMost performance confusion comes from blurring two of them.
Always ask: whose state is it, is it private or shared, and when does it become visible?This question runs from function calls to GPU shared memory.
Name the cost of every mechanismNothing in hardware is free. Area, power and complexity are always in the trade.
