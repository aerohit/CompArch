Connect the modern system

A fast GPU kernel can still leave a slow application. Before it runs, its inputs must be represented, placed in memory, and delivered. After it runs, other devices may need its results. This unit follows that journey outward: from matrix arithmetic and number formats, through shared systems and physical memory, to several GPUs combining their work.

Lesson 25

Matrix hardware and numerical formats

LimitationEven a tiled GPU kernel issues many scalar operations and moves operands repeatedly through its storage hierarchy.
New mechanismMatrix units apply many multiply-adds to a prepared tile, while narrow number formats reduce the bits moved and processed.
New stateOperand fragments spread across threads' registers, accumulator tiles, and scale factors for quantized data.
CostFixed tile shapes and layouts, rounding and range limits, and accuracy that has to be checked rather than assumed.

Recognize a structured operation

Unit 5's tiled multiplication reused values of A and B, but each thread still stepped through individual products for its output. A matrix instruction gives specialized hardware a larger piece of the same regular work:

D = C + A × B
D[i,j] = C[i,j] + Σ_k A[i,k] × B[k,j]// A is M×K, B is K×N, C and D are M×N

Here i selects an output row and j an output column. For that output, k walks across a row of A and down a column of B. For example, D[0,1] in the explorer below is 0 + 1 × 6 + 2 × 8 = 22. The same A value helps form other output columns, and the same B value helps form other output rows. Matrix hardware is built to use each prepared operand for several products.

Press Add next k once and watch every output change, not just D[0,1]. One column of A times one row of B updates the entire small output tile.

The 2 × 2 calculation is one mathematical matrix operation, not necessarily one hardware instruction. Real instructions accept particular shapes and operand types: a common CUDA WMMA shape is 16 × 16 × 16, while other shapes depend on the type and hardware. A 2 × 2 problem might be computed another way or padded into a supported tile. With padding, the hardware performs many products that do not contribute to the 16 useful FLOPs. One instruction therefore tells you neither the useful work nor the number of cycles.

A tile also needs the expected layout. Matrix instructions may divide their operands into fragments held in several threads' registers; one thread's fragment need not be a complete logical row. Instruction families such as wmma and wgmma have different participation and completion rules, so a tuned library is a sensible first performance baseline. Compare a quoted peak only after checking its operand and accumulator types, tile shape, sparsity assumption, and FLOP-counting convention. The next choice is numerical: moving fewer bits can feed the unit faster, but it changes which values the unit can represent.

Range and precision are different problems

A floating-point number stores a sign, a significand (its significant digits), and a power-of-two exponent. More exponent bits usually widen the range of magnitudes. More fraction bits make neighbouring values closer together, improving precision within that range. The table shows why two formats with the same byte count can make very different tradeoffs.

FormatSign / exponent / fraction bitsLargest finite valueGap just above 1
FP321 / 8 / 23≈ 3.40 × 10³⁸2⁻²³
FP161 / 5 / 1065,5042⁻¹⁰
BF161 / 8 / 7≈ 3.39 × 10³⁸2⁻⁷
FP8 E4M3FN1 / 4 / 34482⁻³
FP8 E5M21 / 5 / 257,3442⁻²

FP16 and BF16 both use 2 bytes. Near 1, FP16's gap is 8 times smaller (2⁻¹⁰ versus 2⁻⁷), but BF16's wider exponent range lets it store 70000 coarsely where FP16 overflows. A format choice therefore needs the actual input range and the error the application can tolerate. Even “FP8” is incomplete: E4M3FN and E5M2 split the bits differently and handle overflow differently.

Overflow depends on the rounded result, not just whether the input exceeds the largest finite number: 65505 rounds to FP16's 65504. In the FP8 rows, the toggle changes what happens when a value truly overflows. Non-saturating conversion produces NaN for E4M3FN and infinity for E5M2; saturating conversion clamps to 448 or 57,344. Compare 460 and 470 in E4M3FN: the first rounds to 448, while the second overflows. FP32, FP16 and BF16 also have signed infinities. The explorer models finite inputs; it does not model signed zero, NaN or infinite inputs, or every hardware conversion boundary.

To judge an in-range result, absolute error is |stored − x|. For nonzero x, relative error divides that difference by |x|, so the same absolute error matters more when x is small. At x = 0 the ratio is undefined, and the explorer reports an exact stored zero instead. A tiny nonzero input rounded to zero has 100% relative error, even when its absolute error is tiny. The next figure shows how a format approaches zero before that happens.

The bottom of the range: subnormals

A normal FP16 value has an implicit leading 1 before its 10 stored fraction bits, for 11 significant bits. Its smallest positive value is 2⁻¹⁴ (about 6.1 × 10⁻⁵). Smaller nonzero values are subnormal: they drop the leading 1 and keep a fixed spacing of 2⁻²⁴. That sequence reaches 2⁻²⁴ itself (about 6 × 10⁻⁸), then zero.

That smooth transition describes FP16's representation, not a promise about execution speed. FP8 formats have their own encodings, and some hardware flushes tiny subnormal results to zero or handles them at a different speed. Check the behaviour of the format and machine you intend to use.

Accumulation is another numerical choice

Converting an input and adding products to a running total are two separate rounding decisions. Matrix units often multiply narrow inputs but accumulate in a wider format, preserving more of the partial sum. If an input already rounded to zero during conversion, however, a wide accumulator cannot recover it.

Even an FP32 sum depends on the order of additions. Around 2²⁴, adjacent representable FP32 values are two apart. The exact sum 2²⁴ + 1 lies halfway between 2²⁴ and 2²⁴ + 2; round-to-nearest-even chooses 2²⁴ because its least significant significand bit is zero. Here “even” does not mean an even integer: both neighbours are even integers.

Consequently, (2²⁴ + 1) − 2²⁴ evaluates to 0, while 2²⁴ + (1 − 2²⁴) evaluates to 1. A parallel reduction changes the grouping of additions, so it can change the final bits without changing the mathematical sum. Accuracy and reproducibility must be checked alongside speed.

Try it: quantization trades resolution against clipping

Another way to move fewer bits is to represent values as small integers plus a shared scale. This simple b-bit integer quantizer uses symmetric codes from −Q to Q, where Q = 2b−1 − 1 (7 for 4 bits). Choose a positive scale s, then round and clip each input:

q = clip(round_to_nearest_even(x / s), −Q, Q)
x_reconstructed = s × q

At 4 bits and s = 0.5, each step is 0.5 and the largest reconstruction is 7 × 0.5 = 3.5. The outlier 6.2 clips to 3.5, while 0.3 rounds to 0.5. Increase s to 1: 6.2 now rounds to 6 without clipping, but 0.3 rounds to zero. The codes −7…7 use 15 of the 16 possible 4-bit patterns; −8 is left unused here to keep the range symmetric. This is an integer quantizer, not an FP8 emulator: floating-point spacing is not uniform.

One outlier can thus force a scale too coarse for the rest of a tensor. Per-channel or per-block scales adapt to smaller groups of values, at the cost of storing and handling more scales. Some low-precision matrix formats also combine small floating-point elements with block scales. Test representative inputs and the final application result: acceptable error for inference may be unacceptable for training or for an iterative solver that repeatedly accumulates error.

PredictCheck with the explorers.
  1. In the matrix example, what is C plus only the k = 0 contribution? What is the final D, and how many FLOPs is that?
    Show answer

    The k = 0 outer product is [[5, 6], [15, 18]], so C + it = [[15, 6], [15, 28]]. Adding k = 1's [[14, 16], [28, 32]] gives D = [[29, 22], [43, 60]]. Four outputs × two multiply-adds each = 8 multiplies + 8 adds = 16 FLOPs. Two conceptual steps don't mean two hardware cycles.

  2. FP16 and BF16 use the same bytes. Which has finer spacing near 1, by what factor, and which has the wider range?
    Show answer

    FP16 is 8× finer near 1 (2⁻¹⁰ vs 2⁻⁷). BF16 has the far wider range (8 exponent bits vs 5). The comparison is local to one magnitude, not a universal error bound.

  3. With the 4-bit quantizer, reconstruct 6.2 and 0.3 at s = 0.5 and at s = 1. Which removes clipping, and at what cost?
    Show answer

    s = 0.5: 6.2 → 12.4 → 12, clipped to 7 → 3.5 (error 2.7); 0.3 → 0.6 → 1 → 0.5 (error 0.2). s = 1: 6.2 → 6 (error 0.2); 0.3 → 0 → 0 (error 0.3). The larger scale removes the clipping but coarsens the small value.

  4. Can an FP32 accumulator restore a small input that rounded to zero on conversion? Why can reordering an FP32 reduction change its result?
    Show answer

    No: once the input is zero, the information is gone; a wider accumulator only limits further error. Reordering changes which intermediate sums get rounded: 2²⁴ + 1 rounds to 2²⁴, so different groupings of the same numbers can give different answers.

  5. A matrix unit advertises twice the FLOP/s of another. What do you need to know before predicting a speedup?
    Show answer

    The operand and accumulator types, supported tile shapes and layouts, your problem dimensions and how much padding they cause, any sparsity requirement, the workload's data movement and arithmetic intensity, and conversion and launch costs. Plus the peak's FLOP-counting convention. Double the arithmetic ceiling helps only if the workload can use it and is limited by it.

  6. Transfer. An input vector is represented accurately in BF16, yet a long sum over it still loses the small contributions. Would changing only the input format fix it?
    Show answer

    Not necessarily. Input rounding and accumulation rounding are separate. If the inputs are already accurate, the loss happens as the running sum grows: each small term is rounded away against a large partial sum. What helps is a wider accumulator, a different reduction order (pairwise or tree sums, or adding small terms first), or compensated summation.

When evaluating a matrix operation, keep five things apart: input representation, accumulation semantics, tile layout, useful arithmetic, and data movement.

Lesson 26

Heterogeneous systems and shared memory

LimitationNo single kind of processor is efficient at everything: control-heavy code, massive data parallelism and fixed tensor dataflows want different hardware.
New mechanismA system-on-chip joins CPUs, GPUs, NPUs and DMA engines through a shared fabric and memory controllers.
New stateBuffers passed between engines, each with an owner, a physical home, and completion events that mark handoffs.
CostTransfers, format conversions, synchronization, and shared resources (memory bandwidth, power) that engines compete for.

Give each engine a job it does efficiently

The camera pipeline below has several kinds of work. A CPU handles general software and control decisions; a GPU handles broadly parallel work; a neural processing unit (NPU) specializes in supported operations. A direct memory access (DMA) engine can move data without the CPU executing a load/store loop for every element. Each has a useful role, but the fastest engine for one stage need not produce the fastest complete pipeline.

A system-on-chip (SoC) puts these engines, memory interfaces and I/O in one system. Its internal fabric routes their requests to shared memory controllers. Follow the numbered stages in the figure, paying attention to the buffer that passes between them.

What "shared" can mean

The figure places the engines around shared memory, but “shared” does not yet say whether the CPU and GPU see the same bytes at the same time. Four properties are often bundled into that one word:

PropertyWhat it gives youWhat it does not establish on its own
Shared physical memoryEngines use the same pool of DRAMEqual latency or bandwidth, cache coherence, or safe concurrent access
Unified virtual addressingOne address-space scheme for naming allocationsThat every engine may dereference every pointer, or where the data physically is
Coherent accessCaches follow a protocol that keeps shared locations consistentOrdering across different locations, freedom from races, or that queued work has finished
Managed migrationThe runtime or OS moves supported allocations where they're usedThat no movement happens, or that first touch is free

For example, the same pointer value can name an allocation without telling you whether its pages sit near the CPU or GPU. In CUDA, unified virtual addressing and Unified Memory are separate features, and managed memory behaviour varies by platform; check the supported attributes. An IOMMU translates device addresses and enforces permissions. It can make an address usable by a device, but cannot tell you whether a producer has finished writing the bytes at that address.

A handoff is an ownership protocol

Consider the camera frame in the figure. The input engine must finish writing it before the NPU consumes it, and the platform's handoff must make those writes visible to the NPU. While the NPU may still read the frame, its producer must not overwrite the buffer. The buffer can be reused, and the NPU's output passed on, only after the relevant completion event. The same sequence applies when a CPU produces an accelerator's input.

On a non-coherent path, the platform API may also perform cache maintenance at these transitions; a homemade “done” flag is not a substitute. Coherent memory still does not make an unsynchronized simultaneous read and write safe. Coherence, ordering and atomicity answer different questions, as in Unit 4. A completion event also has a limited scope: one operation finishing does not mean every queued operation has finished.

Try it: pipeline independent chunks

Suppose each input chunk must be copied to a device, processed, and copied back. Its own three stages must stay in that order. If copy-in, compute and copy-out have independent engines, however, chunk 2 can be copying in while chunk 1 computes. Enough buffers and bandwidth let that overlap continue through the batch.

The drawn schedule assumes fixed stage times, separate engines, enough buffers and no contention. An asynchronous API call merely returns control to the CPU before the work finishes; it does not prove the stages overlap. Stream dependencies, copy-engine capabilities and host-buffer properties matter. Pinned host pages stay at stable physical locations while a DMA engine transfers them, and the platform may require pinned buffers for genuinely asynchronous host/device copies. Even separate copy engines can interfere if both transfers use one saturated memory interface.

Power is shared too

The engines can compete even when their data paths do not. CPU, GPU and accelerators often share a package's power and thermal budget; raising one engine's activity can reduce the clock another sustains. Dynamic power is roughly proportional to activity × capacitance × voltage² × frequency (Lecture 1's power wall), with leakage added.

For an energy comparison, measure joules for the whole completed task. An NPU may save energy on arithmetic yet require conversions and transfers that erase the gain. A short peak test also misses the clocks a system sustains after it heats up. The next lesson follows another shared resource: the physical path to memory.

PredictCheck the pipeline with the explorer.
  1. CPU and GPU can name an allocation with one virtual address. Does that establish shared physical DRAM, coherent access, or free transfers?
    Show answer

    None of them. One naming scheme can cover allocations in different physical memories. Permissions, coherence, migration behaviour and the physical route each need their own evidence.

  2. A CPU submits a device operation and immediately reuses its input buffer. What dependency is missing, even if memory is coherent?
    Show answer

    Device completion before the buffer is reused. Submission returns while the work is still queued; the device may read the buffer after the CPU has overwritten it. Coherence faithfully delivers the new, wrong bytes. The fix is an ownership rule tied to the completion event.

  3. With 2 / 3 / 1 ms stages, how long do four chunks take serially and pipelined? What are the first-result latency and steady interval?
    Show answer

    Serial 24 ms; pipelined 15 ms. First result at 6 ms, then one every 3 ms: outputs at 6, 9, 12 and 15 ms.

  4. If the copy engines and the computation share one saturated memory interface, which assumption fails? Why is an asynchronous call not evidence of overlap?
    Show answer

    The fixed, independent stage times: when stages contend for one interface, each slows down. An asynchronous call only means the CPU got control back before the work finished. Check dependencies, buffer properties and engine capabilities, and measure a timeline including completion.

  5. An NPU kernel takes 2 ms against the GPU's 5 ms, but adds 4 ms of conversion and transfer that don't overlap. Which route finishes first? What must an energy comparison measure?
    Show answer

    NPU route: 2 + 4 = 6 ms; GPU route: 5 ms. The GPU wins by 1 ms. An energy comparison needs joules for the whole route (compute, transfer, conversion, and system activity) over the full interval, not just kernel time.

  6. Transfer. The CPU and GPU use the same pointer value, but the GPU's first access through it is slow. Which observations would tell apart page migration, remote access and first-use initialization?
    Show answer

    The pointer value alone tells you nothing about location. Migration can produce page-fault or migration events and change the pages' physical placement; later accesses may be faster while they remain local. Remote access can show interconnect traffic without migration, but a remote cache line may also make later reads fast. First-use initialization (zero-filling or allocating pages on first touch) is another one-time cost, even for memory the CPU never wrote. Compare profiler migration events, placement information, interconnect counters and controlled repeated timings; the timing pattern alone cannot distinguish the cases.

Draw a data path, then annotate every handoff with who may use the storage and which event allows the next stage to begin.

Lesson 27

Packaging, HBM, and memory locality

LimitationVery large dies are harder to manufacture economically, while a narrow board-level memory path can starve a large accelerator.
New mechanismChiplets joined in one package, and HBM: stacked DRAM with a very wide interface right next to the processor.
New statePhysical placement: which die, which memory controller, which stack or node holds each page.
CostInterfaces and locality boundaries between dies, limited HBM capacity, and non-uniform access times (NUMA).

A package is a physical arrangement

The SoC in Lesson 26 joined several engines, but it did not say how they are built or where their memory sits. Start with a die, one piece of silicon. A chiplet is a die designed to work with other dies. Their package connects and protects them and attaches them to the board. Dense wiring on an interposer or silicon bridge can connect neighbouring dies; 3D integration stacks dies vertically.

Compare the side and top views below. Both depict short, wide connections between compute and memory within a package. That physical arrangement helps determine which transfers are cheap and which must cross a narrower or more distant link.

Smaller dies can improve manufacturing yield: a defect affects a smaller piece of silicon. Chiplets also let designers reuse parts or manufacture different functions with different processes. The tradeoff is more interfaces and assembly work, and sometimes new locality boundaries. “Chiplet” describes construction, not the number of processors or memory domains that software sees. A standard die-to-die interface such as UCIe does not make every chiplet design interchangeable or every route equally fast.

Try it: where HBM's bandwidth comes from

High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) stacks DRAM dies and places a very wide interface close to the processor. An interposer can carry many parallel wires that would be impractical to route across a board. Width raises potential bandwidth; it does not make DRAM behave like a register file. First calculate the raw ceiling from the number of data pins and the rate on each pin:

The four bandwidths add only when requests can be spread across the stacks. If one hot allocation is served mostly by one stack, the other three interfaces do little for it. Actual throughput also falls below the raw ceiling because controllers schedule accesses, DRAM refreshes, requests may lack locality, and other traffic competes for the path. More capacity per stack would help a larger problem fit without changing that pin-based bandwidth calculation.

Bandwidth is also different from the latency of one dependent, cache-missing load. That load must make its way through caches, the on-chip fabric, a controller queue and DRAM before the next dependent address is known. Placement affects that journey. The NUMA example makes the cost of an extra physical hop visible.

Try it: locality depends on who is asking

In a NUMA (non-uniform memory access) system, memory cost depends on where the requester and the data are located. A core can reach its own node's controller directly, but reaches another node's through a fabric. The virtual address need not change; physical placement changes the route.

Our two-node teaching machine puts a thread on A. A cache-missing load from A's DRAM takes 80 ns; one from B's takes 140 ns. The thread is pointer chasing: each load reveals the address of the next, so it cannot send several requests at once. Move the slider to change the fraction of these dependent loads that travel to B.

Allocation and execution must agree. Under a common “first touch” policy, the thread that first writes a page determines its initial node. A successful malloc alone therefore does not place the page beside its eventual worker. If a setup thread on B initializes an array and a worker on A later pointer-chases through it, that worker may pay the 140 ns remote path on every cache miss.

Having the eventual workers initialize their own pages can help, but explicit memory policies, a full node or later migration may change the result. Pinning a worker to A does not move pages already on B; migration itself costs time. To investigate, connect the worker's placement and initialization history to the pages' measured placement, then to the memory traffic you observe.

The same route-based reasoning applies beyond CPU nodes: memory near one GPU can be remote to another. For a frequently accessed allocation, draw requester → caches → fabric → controller → memory and mark what else shares each hop. Packaging makes some hops short; it does not erase the need to find where the bytes actually live.

PredictCheck with the explorers.
  1. For the four-stack example, what are the raw bandwidth and capacity? Why not multiply the pin rate by two?
    Show answer

    Per stack 1024 × 4 × 10⁹ / 8 = 512 GB/s; four stacks 2,048 GB/s and 64 GB. The rate was given as transferred bits per second per pin, so it already counts both clock edges. Doubling it double-counts.

  2. A thread on A sends 25% of its dependent cache-missing reads to B. What are the mean latency and the time for a million reads?
    Show answer

    0.75 × 80 + 0.25 × 140 = 95 ns; a million serialized reads take 95 ms.

  3. Why doesn't that calculation predict the bandwidth of a loop with many independent reads? Name two resources that might limit it.
    Show answer

    Dependent loads allow one request at a time, so latency dominates. Independent reads put many requests in flight and hit a throughput limit instead: memory controllers and DRAM channels, the inter-node link, cache interfaces, or the number of outstanding misses the core can track.

  4. Does moving a worker from B to A move its already-allocated pages? How could initialization and policy change the outcome?
    Show answer

    No, existing pages stay put unless something migrates them. Under first touch, whoever initializes a page decides its home, so having the eventual worker initialize its own data puts it locally. Explicit policies, fallback and migration also play in; check the actual page placement.

  5. A processor goes from one die to four chiplets. What evidence would you need before claiming four NUMA domains, or a fourfold speedup?
    Show answer

    For NUMA: where the memory controllers are, the inter-die fabric, the cache organization, coherence domains, address interleaving, and what topology the OS exposes. For speedup: parallelism in the workload, usable execution capacity, bandwidth and communication costs. Neither follows from the die count.

  6. Transfer. A workload only fits after HBM capacity is added, while raw interface bandwidth stays the same. Why can its runtime still improve drastically?
    Show answer

    Because the path changes, not the bandwidth. Before, data that didn't fit had to be staged or spilled over a much slower link (host memory over PCIe, or recomputation). Once everything fits, those transfers disappear. Capacity and bandwidth are separate dimensions, but capacity decides which bandwidth the data has to use.

Explain a memory cost from the physical route and the access pattern. Treat package boundaries and software-visible locality domains as related but separate facts.

Lesson 28

Multiple GPUs and nodes

LimitationOne accelerator's memory and compute run out, but splitting work across several means their partial results must be combined.
New mechanismCollective operations (all-reduce, all-gather, …) implemented by algorithms such as the ring, over direct GPU links and networks.
New stateRanks, communication buffers, and per-round schedules that all participants must agree on.
CostCommunication time that doesn't shrink as fast as compute, shared physical links, and exposed synchronization.

Partition the work, then account for its edges

Adding GPUs gives a job more compute and memory, but any partition whose result depends on another must exchange data. First specify what “more GPUs” means. Strong scaling keeps the total work fixed and gives each GPU a smaller share. Weak scaling keeps each GPU's share roughly fixed and grows the total job. The same communication cost has a different effect in those two experiments.

In data-parallel training, GPUs process different examples and combine their gradient contributions. If each GPU reports a mean over an equally sized local batch, the sum of those means divided by the GPU count is the mean over the combined batch. Other partitions move different data: tensor partitioning splits an operation within a layer, while pipeline partitioning gives different layers to different devices and passes activations between them. These methods can be combined, but each creates its own communication dependencies.

Communication libraries call a participant a rank, a software identity that here corresponds to one GPU. Ranks taking part in a collective must agree on the calls' order, sizes, data types and operations. Before choosing an algorithm or link, decide which rank needs which result.

Know what each collective promises

Read the diagram from inputs to outputs. Broadcast copies one rank's data to the others. Reduce combines contributions at one root; all-reduce gives the combined result to every rank. Reduce-scatter leaves each rank with a different completed piece, while all-gather distributes pieces until everyone has the full collection. The result each operation promises is its semantics; how bytes move to produce it is a separate choice.

One collective can have several implementations. The ring below uses reduce-scatter followed by all-gather; a library such as NCCL may choose another algorithm based on message size and physical topology. The promised output stays the same. It also matters when that output may be used: enqueueing work, completing work, and making its result available to a dependent consumer are distinct events.

With CUDA and NCCL, the host call returning means the collective was enqueued. A kernel launched later in the same stream is ordered after it; a consumer in another stream needs an explicit dependency such as an event. Completion is per rank and stream, not a claim that all ranks finish at the same instant or that the system reached a global barrier.

Try it: a ring all-reduce

Four ranks form a directed ring R0 → R1 → R2 → R3 → R0. Their starting vectors are R0 = [1, 2, 3, 4], R1 = [10, 20, 30, 40], R2 = [100, 200, 300, 400] and R3 = [1000, 2000, 3000, 4000]. Each vector has four 4-byte elements, divided into four one-element chunks. The first desired output is 1 + 10 + 100 + 1000 = 1111; the full result is [1111, 2222, 3333, 4444].

The ring reaches that result in two phases. During three reduce-scatter rounds, each rank sends a partial chunk to its neighbour, which adds its own contribution. Each rank ends with one completed chunk. During three all-gather rounds, the completed chunks circulate until everyone has all four. Step through the explorer and watch the contribution labels as well as the values. Every send in a round uses the state from before that round.

Each rank began with 4 × 4 = 16 bytes and sent one 4-byte chunk in each of six rounds: 24 bytes sent per rank. This counts bytes on the ring, not arithmetic additions. The benefit is that no rank has to send its entire vector in every round.

Derive the communication cost

Replace the four ranks by P ranks, each contributing an M-byte vector. Splitting each vector into P equal chunks makes a send carry M/P bytes. There are P − 1 rounds in each of the two phases, so each rank sends that chunk size 2(P − 1) times:

bytes sent per rank = 2 × (P − 1)/P × M
T_ring ≈ 2 × (P − 1) × α + 2 × (P − 1)/P × M / β

Here α is a fixed startup cost per round and β is the bandwidth of each directed ring link. The model assumes equal chunks, identical full-duplex links, no competing traffic and negligible addition time. Sending and receiving can happen together, so the receive time is not added again. For P = 4 and M = 100 MB, each chunk is 25 MB. Six sends total 150 MB per rank. At β = 25 GB/s and α = 5 μs, that is 6 ms of transfer plus 6 × 5 μs of startup, or 6.03 ms.

Now keep the vector size fixed while adding ranks. Compute per rank may shrink, but each rank sends 2(P−1)/P × M bytes, a quantity that rises toward 2M. The bandwidth term approaches 2M/β from below, and the startup term 2(P−1)α continues to grow. Even this ideal ring's communication time therefore increases with rank count:

RanksBandwidth termStartup termAll-reduce time
46.00 ms0.03 ms6.03 ms
87.00 ms0.07 ms7.07 ms
167.50 ms0.15 ms7.65 ms

Those are model predictions, not measured times. Real runs can pay for shared links, different chunk scheduling and software overhead. The next explorer adds idealized compute time to show what that communication does to speedup.

A logical ring still runs on physical links

The ring formula gives every directed edge its own β. Physical machines may route several such edges through one bottleneck, such as a network card or oversubscribed switch port. Those edges then compete for the same capacity. A direct GPU link, a PCIe switch, a CPU interconnect and a network hop can have very different bandwidth, latency and contention.

When using a quoted bandwidth, ask whether it is per link or aggregate, one direction or both summed, raw line rate or useful payload, and inside one node or across a network. A “direct” device-to-device route avoids staging through CPU memory, yet it still needs mappings, permissions, synchronization and transfer time. Directness alone does not promise coherence.

Overlap follows the dependency graph

The scaling bars place all communication after all computation. Training software can instead split gradients into buckets and reduce an early bucket while later gradients are still being computed. A bucket cannot travel before its values exist, and a consumer cannot use its reduced result before completion. That leaves the final ready bucket's communication at least partly exposed in many schedules. Concurrent communication may also compete with compute for memory bandwidth.

For a fixed amount of compute and communication, their sum describes full serialization; max(compute, communication) is an optimistic lower bound if they overlap perfectly. Neither tells you what an actual program achieves. Inspect a timeline through final synchronization, measure the whole iteration, and recheck numerical results if the reduction order changes.

  1. After the three reduce-scatter rounds, does every rank have the complete result? Which complete chunk does each own?
    Show answer

    No, each has just one complete chunk: R0 has chunk 1 = 2222, R1 chunk 2 = 3333, R2 chunk 3 = 4444, R3 chunk 0 = 1111. The all-gather rounds distribute them.

  2. Four ranks, 100 MB each: how many bytes does each rank send across both phases? What's the modelled time at β = 25 GB/s, α = 5 μs?
    Show answer

    2 × 3/4 × 100 MB = 150 MB, taking 6 ms, plus 6 rounds × 5 μs = 0.03 ms: 6.03 ms. Each rank also receives 150 MB, at the same time over the full-duplex links, so that isn't added again.

  3. With 40 ms of total compute, what are the runtime, speedup and efficiency at four ranks in the blocking model? And at eight?
    Show answer

    P = 4: 10 + 6.03 = 16.03 ms, speedup ≈ 2.50×, efficiency ≈ 62%. P = 8: 5 + 7 + 0.07 = 12.07 ms, ≈ 3.31×, ≈ 41%. Compute divides perfectly here; communication doesn't.

  4. Two logical ring edges share one physical link with 25 GB/s aggregate capacity. Why is the model optimistic? Does a "direct" path remove the need to wait for completion?
    Show answer

    The model gave each edge its own 25 GB/s; two edges sharing a link split it, so transfers slow or queue. A direct path skips a CPU buffer but still takes time, and anyone consuming the data or reusing the buffer must still wait for completion.

  5. A collective needs a gradient bucket that hasn't been computed yet. Can launching it asynchronously overlap that bucket's production with its transfer? How can earlier buckets still overlap?
    Show answer

    No: data can't be sent before it exists; an async launch just queues behind the dependency. But buckets that are already finished can be reduced while later buckets are computed, subject to contention. The last bucket's communication often remains on the critical path.

  6. Transfer. In the 4-rank ring, the edges R0 → R1 and R2 → R3 share one physical link whose aggregate capacity is 25 GB/s across both directions. For M = 100 MB, what lower bound does that shared link put on the all-reduce's transfer time?
    Show answer

    Each ring edge carries 2 × 3/4 × 100 MB = 150 MB. Both count against the same 25 GB/s aggregate capacity, so at least 300 MB crosses that bottleneck: 300 MB ÷ 25 GB/s = 12 ms, before the α terms. If the link instead provided an independent 25 GB/s in each direction and the flows used opposite directions, they could overlap and this particular transfer lower bound would be 6 ms. Mark both the route and the capacity convention before adding bytes.

Before quoting a bandwidth or claiming a scaling result, draw the collective's data dependencies and the physical routes it uses.

Putting it together

Trace one hypothetical training iteration. A CPU prepares input in host memory. Four GPUs each receive a chunk, run low-precision matrix operations with a specified accumulator, combine FP32 gradient elements (4 bytes each) with an all-reduce, then return a compact result to the CPU.

  1. Name the representations. Input elements, scale metadata, accumulators, communication buffers, output conversion. Quantizing the inputs doesn't shrink an FP32 gradient buffer.
  2. Name the physical homes. Which CPU node holds the host data, which GPU owns each device allocation. A shared pointer name isn't a placement.
  3. Draw every movement. Host → GPU, HBM → on-chip tiles, GPU → neighbour GPU, GPU → host. One byte can cross several boundaries, each with its own count.
  4. Establish the handoffs. Input produced before transfer; transfer before use; local gradients before the reduction; reduction complete before its result is used; output transfer complete before the CPU reads it.
  5. Find the critical path. Overlap independent chunks only where engines and dependencies allow, and include initialization, conversion, launches, transfers, collectives and synchronization in the interval you optimize.
  6. Validate. Compare with a numerical reference, then measure steady-state and first-use behaviour separately. A faster wrong answer doesn't count.
One segment, worked out

With 10 ms of local compute per GPU and a 100 MB gradient all-reduced over the lesson's 25 GB/s ring, the compute-then-communicate segment takes 10 + 6.03 = 16.03 ms. That's not the whole iteration: host transfers, conversions and the CPU's consumption of the output are extra stages. To improve it, find the largest exposed stage and test one hypothesis: fewer bytes, better locality, cheaper conversion, faster useful compute, or legal overlap. Transfer exercise: annotate a path and a time budget

A fresh hypothetical setup, with its own numbers. One CPU node holds a 400 MB input batch in host memory. Four GPUs each copy their 100 MB share over their own 25 GB/s host link (assume the links and host memory don't contend). Each GPU computes for 12 ms and produces a 200 MB FP32 gradient, which is all-reduced over a ring with β = 50 GB/s per link and α = 10 μs. Each GPU then returns a 4 MB result to the host over its 25 GB/s link, and the CPU reads it. Assume the result is proportional to the local input (16 MB in total for the batch) and that local compute time scales in proportion to the local batch. Assume every stage waits for the previous one: no overlap.

  1. Draw where the input, the gradients and the outputs live, and mark every copy and every completion dependency.
  2. Compute each stage's time and the critical-path estimate for one iteration.
  3. Name the bottleneck, and propose one measurement that could disprove your prediction.
  4. Now use 8 GPUs. Compare strong scaling (same 400 MB batch) with weak scaling (each GPU keeps 100 MB, so the batch doubles). The gradient stays 200 MB because it depends on the model, not the batch.
Show a worked answer

Path. Host memory on the CPU's node → (copy, then completion) → each GPU's HBM → compute → gradient in HBM → ring all-reduce over GPU links (each rank's consumer waits for that rank's all-reduce to complete, not for a global barrier) → 4 MB result copied to host memory → the CPU may read only after that copy completes.

Stages. Copy in: 100 MB ÷ 25 GB/s = 4 ms. Compute: 12 ms. All-reduce: 2 × 3/4 × 200 MB ÷ 50 GB/s = 6 ms, plus 6 × 10 μs = 0.06 ms, so 6.06 ms. Copy out: 4 MB ÷ 25 GB/s = 0.16 ms. Critical path: 4 + 12 + 6.06 + 0.16 = 22.22 ms.

Bottleneck. Compute is the largest stage (54%), then the all-reduce (27%). The obvious improvement is overlapping gradient buckets' communication with the rest of the backward pass, which could hide much of the 6.06 ms; the final ready bucket leaves a communication tail that can't be hidden behind earlier backward-pass computation in this schedule, though other independent work or a different schedule could change how much stays exposed.

A disproving measurement. Profile one iteration's timeline (for example with Nsight Systems) and time the all-reduce by itself at 200 MB. If it takes much longer than 6.06 ms, the ring's links are shared or slower than assumed, and the "compute is the bottleneck" conclusion may be wrong too.

8 GPUs, strong scaling. Copy in 50 MB = 2 ms; compute 6 ms (assuming it halves with the batch); all-reduce 2 × 7/8 × 200 MB ÷ 50 GB/s = 7 ms + 14 × 10 μs, so 7.14 ms; copy out 2 MB = 0.08 ms (the 16 MB result, split eight ways). (If instead each GPU still returned a fixed 4 MB, copy-out would stay 0.16 ms and the total would be 15.30 ms, about 1.45×.) Total 15.22 ms: a speedup of 22.22 / 15.22 ≈ 1.46× on twice the GPUs, because the all-reduce grew while everything else halved.

8 GPUs, weak scaling. Copy in 4 ms, compute 12 ms, all-reduce 7.14 ms, copy out 0.16 ms: 23.30 ms for an 800 MB batch. Throughput goes from 400/22.22 ≈ 18.0 to 800/23.30 ≈ 34.3 MB of input per ms, about 1.91×. Weak scaling looks better because each GPU's compute share stays large compared with the communication.
