Connect the modern system

A fast kernel is one stage of a bigger computation. Its inputs have a numerical format. They live in some physical memory, arrive over a finite path, and its results may have to be combined with other devices'. This unit follows that chain outward from the GPU core: into matrix units and number formats, across a system-on-chip, through packages and memory stacks, and out to many GPUs working together.

Tell apart the different things "shared memory" can mean in a heterogeneous system, and schedule a transfer–compute pipeline. Estimate HBM bandwidth and NUMA latency from first principles. Trace a ring all-reduce and explain why multi-GPU scaling falls short.

Lesson 25

Matrix hardware and numerical formats

LimitationScalar multiply-adds, even across many lanes, spend most of their energy fetching and moving operands rather than doing arithmetic.
New mechanismMatrix units perform a whole small matrix multiply-accumulate per instruction, and narrow number formats move and multiply fewer bits.
New stateOperand fragments spread across threads' registers, accumulator tiles, and scale factors for quantized data.
CostFixed tile shapes and layouts, rounding and range limits, and accuracy that has to be checked rather than assumed.

Recognize a structured operation

In Unit 5, each thread kept a running sum while its block reused tiles. A matrix instruction hands the hardware a bigger piece of that regular structure at once:

D = C + A × B
D[i,j] = C[i,j] + Σ_k A[i,k] × B[k,j]// A is M×K, B is K×N, C and D are M×N

Every value of A contributes to several output columns, and every value of B to several output rows. A dedicated datapath can exploit that reuse: once an operand is in the unit, it feeds many multiplies. The instruction's shape and operand types say what work is offered; they don't reveal the circuit, its cycle count or its energy.

The operands also have a layout, not just values. A matrix instruction expects its tile in specific registers or memory, often split across the threads of a warp as fragments: each thread holds a piece that need not be a neat row of the logical matrix. The rules differ between instruction families (NVIDIA's warp-level wmma, warp-group wgmma and newer single-thread-initiated forms each have their own participation and completion rules). For applications, a tuned matrix library is the right first baseline. And a quoted matrix peak must name its precision, sparsity assumption and FLOP-counting convention before it means anything for your workload. A narrow or oddly shaped problem may waste much of a fixed tile on padding.

Range and precision are different problems

A floating-point number is a sign, a significand near 1, and a power-of-two exponent. More exponent bits widen the range: how large and how small a value can be. More fraction bits refine the precision: how finely values are spaced within a given range. Neither makes real arithmetic exact.

FormatSign / exponent / fraction bitsLargest finite valueGap just above 1
FP321 / 8 / 23≈ 3.40 × 10³⁸2⁻²³
FP161 / 5 / 1065,5042⁻¹⁰
BF161 / 8 / 7≈ 3.39 × 10³⁸2⁻⁷
FP8 E4M3FN1 / 4 / 34482⁻³
FP8 E5M21 / 5 / 257,3442⁻²

FP16 and BF16 both take 2 bytes, but behave differently. Near 1, FP16's spacing is 8 times finer (2⁻¹⁰ versus 2⁻⁷), while BF16 keeps FP32's 8-bit exponent and therefore its huge range. Try 70000: FP16 overflows, BF16 stores it coarsely. Choosing a format means looking at the actual values and the tolerable error, not just the byte count. "FP8" alone isn't even a complete description: E4M3 and E5M2 split the bits differently and treat infinities differently.

The bottom of the range: subnormals

A normal FP16 number has an implicit leading 1 in front of its 10 stored fraction bits, giving 11 significant bits. The smallest positive normal value is 2⁻¹⁴ (about 6.1 × 10⁻⁵). Below that, the format switches to subnormal numbers: the leading 1 is dropped and the spacing stays fixed at 2⁻²⁴, all the way down to the smallest positive value, 2⁻²⁴ itself (about 6 × 10⁻⁸), and then zero.

Accumulation is another numerical choice

Matrix units commonly take low-precision inputs but accumulate in a wider format, which keeps more of the running sum than rounding every partial sum to the narrow type. But a wider accumulator cannot recover information lost when the inputs were converted. If a small input rounded to zero, it is gone.

Order matters even in FP32. With round-to-nearest-even, 2²⁴ + 1 rounds back to 2²⁴ (the next FP32 value above 2²⁴ is 2²⁴ + 2, and 1 is exactly halfway, a tie that goes to the even neighbour). So (2²⁴ + 1) − 2²⁴ gives 0, while 2²⁴ + (1 − 2²⁴) gives 1. A parallel reduction groups additions differently from a sequential loop, so its last bits can differ. Reproducibility, accuracy and speed are related requirements, but not the same one.

Try it: quantization trades resolution against clipping

A simple integer quantizer with b bits uses symmetric codes from −Q to Q, where Q = 2b−1 − 1 (7 for 4 bits). With a positive scale s:

q = clip(round_to_nearest_even(x / s), −Q, Q)
x_reconstructed = s × q

A single outlier can force a large scale that wastes resolution on everything else in a tensor. Per-channel or per-block scales adapt to local ranges, at the cost of storing and handling the scales. Modern low-precision matrix formats combine small floating-point elements with block scales, and those scales are part of the format's contract. Judge accuracy with representative data, both absolute and relative error, and the downstream result that matters. What's tolerable for inference may not be for training, or for an iterative solver that accumulates error.

PredictCheck with the explorers.
  1. In the matrix example, what is C plus only the k = 0 contribution? What is the final D, and how many FLOPs is that?
    Show answer

    The k = 0 outer product is [[5, 6], [15, 18]], so C + it = [[15, 6], [15, 28]]. Adding k = 1's [[14, 16], [28, 32]] gives D = [[29, 22], [43, 60]]. Four outputs × two multiply-adds each = 8 multiplies + 8 adds = 16 FLOPs. Two conceptual steps don't mean two hardware cycles.

  2. FP16 and BF16 use the same bytes. Which has finer spacing near 1, by what factor, and which has the wider range?
    Show answer

    FP16 is 8× finer near 1 (2⁻¹⁰ vs 2⁻⁷). BF16 has the far wider range (8 exponent bits vs 5). The comparison is local to one magnitude, not a universal error bound.

  3. With the 4-bit quantizer, reconstruct 6.2 and 0.3 at s = 0.5 and at s = 1. Which removes clipping, and at what cost?
    Show answer

    s = 0.5: 6.2 → 12.4 → 12, clipped to 7 → 3.5 (error 2.7); 0.3 → 0.6 → 1 → 0.5 (error 0.2). s = 1: 6.2 → 6 (error 0.2); 0.3 → 0 → 0 (error 0.3). The larger scale removes the clipping but coarsens the small value.

  4. Can an FP32 accumulator restore a small input that rounded to zero on conversion? Why can reordering an FP32 reduction change its result?
    Show answer

    No: once the input is zero, the information is gone; a wider accumulator only limits further error. Reordering changes which intermediate sums get rounded: 2²⁴ + 1 rounds to 2²⁴, so different groupings of the same numbers can give different answers.

  5. A matrix unit advertises twice the FLOP/s of another. What do you need to know before predicting a speedup?
    Show answer

    The operand and accumulator types, supported tile shapes and layouts, your problem dimensions and how much padding they cause, any sparsity requirement, the workload's data movement and arithmetic intensity, and conversion and launch costs. Plus the peak's FLOP-counting convention. Double the arithmetic ceiling helps only if the workload can use it and is limited by it.

  6. Transfer. An input vector is represented accurately in BF16, yet a long sum over it still loses the small contributions. Would changing only the input format fix it?
    Show answer

    Not necessarily. Input rounding and accumulation rounding are separate. If the inputs are already accurate, the loss happens as the running sum grows: each small term is rounded away against a large partial sum. What helps is a wider accumulator, a different reduction order (pairwise or tree sums, or adding small terms first), or compensated summation.

When evaluating a matrix operation, keep five things apart: input representation, accumulation semantics, tile layout, useful arithmetic, and data movement.

Lesson 26

Heterogeneous systems and shared memory

LimitationNo single kind of processor is efficient at everything: control-heavy code, massive data parallelism and fixed tensor dataflows want different hardware.
New mechanismA system-on-chip joins CPUs, GPUs, NPUs and DMA engines through a shared fabric and memory controllers.
New stateBuffers passed between engines, each with an owner, a physical home, and completion events that mark handoffs.
CostTransfers, format conversions, synchronization, and shared resources (memory bandwidth, power) that engines compete for.

Give each engine a job it does efficiently

A CPU handles control-heavy work and general software; a GPU offers broad parallel throughput; an NPU or other accelerator specializes in a supported set of operations; a DMA engine copies data without the CPU running a load/store loop for every element. These are roles, not a ranking. A system-on-chip (SoC) integrates such engines with memory interfaces and I/O, and its internal fabric routes requests and responses between them. Shared controllers and links become bottlenecks when several engines are busy at once.

What "shared" can mean

"The CPU and GPU share memory" can mean several different things, and they come in different combinations:

PropertyWhat it gives youWhat it does not establish on its own
Shared physical memoryEngines use the same pool of DRAMEqual latency or bandwidth, cache coherence, or safe concurrent access
Unified virtual addressingOne address-space scheme for naming allocationsThat every engine may dereference every pointer, or where the data physically is
Coherent accessCaches follow a protocol that keeps shared locations consistentOrdering across different locations, freedom from races, or that queued work has finished
Managed migrationThe runtime or OS moves supported allocations where they're usedThat no movement happens, or that first touch is free

In CUDA, for example, unified virtual addressing and Unified Memory are separate features, and managed memory behaves differently depending on the platform: query the attributes rather than assuming. An IOMMU translates device addresses and enforces access permissions, which answers where the device may access. It doesn't publish the CPU's latest writes or say a producer has finished.

A handoff is an ownership protocol

A CPU fills a buffer; a device consumes it. The steps: finish producing the bytes; submit the work using the platform's mechanism, which makes those writes visible to the device; don't touch the buffer while the device uses it; and wait for the device's completion event before reading its output or reusing the buffer. On a non-coherent path, cache maintenance is part of those transitions, done through the platform API rather than a home-made flag. Even coherent memory doesn't make an unsynchronized simultaneous read and write safe: as in Unit 4, coherence, ordering and atomicity answer different questions. And a completion event has a scope: one operation finishing doesn't mean the whole system is idle.

Try it: pipeline independent chunks

Split the data into chunks. Each chunk needs a host-to-device transfer, a computation, and a device-to-host transfer. If the three stages run on independent engines (two copy engines and the compute units), with enough buffers and bandwidth, different chunks can be in different stages at once.

On a real system, asynchronous API calls alone don't guarantee overlap: stream dependencies, whether host memory is pinned, and the hardware's copy engines all matter, and two engines drawing on one saturated memory interface interfere even if their bars overlap on a timeline.

Power is shared too

CPU, GPU and specialized engines often share one package's power and thermal budget, so pushing one harder can lower the clock another can sustain. Dynamic power is roughly proportional to activity × capacitance × voltage² × frequency (Lecture 1's power wall), plus leakage. For efficiency comparisons, measure joules for the whole completed task, not just time: a specialized engine may save arithmetic energy but add a costly conversion or transfer. And a short peak test doesn't describe sustained operation under a thermal limit.

PredictCheck the pipeline with the explorer.
  1. CPU and GPU can name an allocation with one virtual address. Does that establish shared physical DRAM, coherent access, or free transfers?
    Show answer

    None of them. One naming scheme can cover allocations in different physical memories. Permissions, coherence, migration behaviour and the physical route each need their own evidence.

  2. A CPU submits a device operation and immediately reuses its input buffer. What dependency is missing, even if memory is coherent?
    Show answer

    Device completion before the buffer is reused. Submission returns while the work is still queued; the device may read the buffer after the CPU has overwritten it. Coherence faithfully delivers the new, wrong bytes. The fix is an ownership rule tied to the completion event.

  3. With 2 / 3 / 1 ms stages, how long do four chunks take serially and pipelined? What are the first-result latency and steady interval?
    Show answer

    Serial 24 ms; pipelined 15 ms. First result at 6 ms, then one every 3 ms: outputs at 6, 9, 12 and 15 ms.

  4. If the copy engines and the computation share one saturated memory interface, which assumption fails? Why is an asynchronous call not evidence of overlap?
    Show answer

    The fixed, independent stage times: when stages contend for one interface, each slows down. An asynchronous call only means the CPU got control back before the work finished. Check dependencies, buffer properties and engine capabilities, and measure a timeline including completion.

  5. An NPU kernel takes 2 ms against the GPU's 5 ms, but adds 4 ms of conversion and transfer that don't overlap. Which route finishes first? What must an energy comparison measure?
    Show answer

    NPU route: 2 + 4 = 6 ms; GPU route: 5 ms. The GPU wins by 1 ms. An energy comparison needs joules for the whole route (compute, transfer, conversion, and system activity) over the full interval, not just kernel time.

  6. Transfer. The CPU and GPU use the same pointer value, but the GPU's first access through it is slow. Which observations would tell apart page migration, remote access and first-use initialization?
    Show answer

    The pointer value alone tells you nothing about location. Migration shows up as page-fault or migration events in a profiler and fast second accesses once the pages have moved. Remote access stays slow on every access and shows traffic over the interconnect, with no migration events. First-use initialization (zero-filling or allocating pages on first touch) is a one-time cost that also appears for memory the CPU never wrote. Collect evidence (profiler events, repeated timings, interconnect counters) before inferring where the bytes live.

Draw a data path, then annotate every handoff with who may use the storage and which event allows the next stage to begin.

Lesson 27

Packaging, HBM, and memory locality

LimitationHuge single dies yield poorly, and a conventional board-level memory bus can't supply the bytes a big accelerator consumes.
New mechanismChiplets joined in one package, and HBM: stacked DRAM with a very wide interface right next to the processor.
New statePhysical placement: which die, which memory controller, which stack or node holds each page.
CostInterfaces and locality boundaries between dies, limited HBM capacity, and non-uniform access times (NUMA).

A package is a physical arrangement

A die is one piece of silicon. A chiplet is a die designed as a building block of a larger design. A package connects and protects one or more dies and attaches them to the board. An interposer or silicon bridge provides dense wiring between neighbouring dies, and 3D integration stacks dies vertically. These choices change the distance, density, cost, power and bandwidth of communication.

Splitting a design into chiplets helps manufacturing yield (a defect ruins a small die, not a huge one), lets parts be reused, and allows each function its own process technology. It also adds interfaces, assembly complexity and possibly locality boundaries. "Chiplet" describes how a chip is built; it doesn't say whether software sees one processor or several memory domains. Standards such as UCIe define a common die-to-die interface; they don't make every chiplet design interchangeable or every path equally fast.

Try it: where HBM's bandwidth comes from

High Bandwidth Memory stacks DRAM dies and connects them through a very wide interface placed right beside the processor. Many parallel wires that would be impractical to route across a board become practical on an interposer. A memory controller still schedules accesses, and workloads still need locality and plenty of requests in flight. HBM is fast, capacity-limited memory, not a giant register file. Bandwidth is simple arithmetic from pins and rates:

Capacity and bandwidth are separate dimensions: more gigabytes per stack doesn't add bandwidth, and high bandwidth doesn't make one dependent read faster. HBM's wide interface raises the bandwidth ceiling, but it doesn't by itself promise low latency for a dependent load. That load still travels through the cache hierarchy, the on-chip interconnect and the memory controller's queues to the DRAM and back, and contention and data placement affect every step. The next section shows placement at work.

Try it: locality depends on who is asking

In a NUMA (non-uniform memory access) system, the cost of reaching memory depends on where the requester sits relative to the memory. A core reaches its own node's controller directly, or crosses a fabric to another node's. The virtual address doesn't change; the physical placement decides the route. Our teaching machine has two nodes. A thread on node A takes 80 ns for a cache-missing load from A's DRAM and 140 ns from B's. Each load's address depends on the previous result (pointer chasing), so loads can't overlap.

Allocation and execution must agree. Under the common "first touch" policy, the thread that first writes a page decides its initial node, so a successful malloc doesn't mean the pages ended up near the thread that will use them. Explicit policies, fallback when a node is full, shared zero pages and migration all complicate that picture. A practical investigation connects worker placement, allocation policy, initialization, measured page placement and later accesses. Initializing data in parallel by the eventual workers often helps; pinning a worker to a node without placing its data leaves the remote route in place; migrating pages costs time too. The same reasoning applies to accelerators: memory local to one GPU is remote to another. For every hot allocation, draw requester → caches → fabric → controller → memory, and mark what else shares each hop.

PredictCheck with the explorers.
  1. For the four-stack example, what are the raw bandwidth and capacity? Why not multiply the pin rate by two?
    Show answer

    Per stack 1024 × 4 × 10⁹ / 8 = 512 GB/s; four stacks 2,048 GB/s and 64 GB. The rate was given as transferred bits per second per pin, so it already counts both clock edges. Doubling it double-counts.

  2. A thread on A sends 25% of its dependent cache-missing reads to B. What are the mean latency and the time for a million reads?
    Show answer

    0.75 × 80 + 0.25 × 140 = 95 ns; a million serialized reads take 95 ms.

  3. Why doesn't that calculation predict the bandwidth of a loop with many independent reads? Name two resources that might limit it.
    Show answer

    Dependent loads allow one request at a time, so latency dominates. Independent reads put many requests in flight and hit a throughput limit instead: memory controllers and DRAM channels, the inter-node link, cache interfaces, or the number of outstanding misses the core can track.

  4. Does moving a worker from B to A move its already-allocated pages? How could initialization and policy change the outcome?
    Show answer

    No, existing pages stay put unless something migrates them. Under first touch, whoever initializes a page decides its home, so having the eventual worker initialize its own data puts it locally. Explicit policies, fallback and migration also play in; check the actual page placement.

  5. A processor goes from one die to four chiplets. What evidence would you need before claiming four NUMA domains, or a fourfold speedup?
    Show answer

    For NUMA: where the memory controllers are, the inter-die fabric, the cache organization, coherence domains, address interleaving, and what topology the OS exposes. For speedup: parallelism in the workload, usable execution capacity, bandwidth and communication costs. Neither follows from the die count.

  6. Transfer. A workload only fits after HBM capacity is added, while raw interface bandwidth stays the same. Why can its runtime still improve drastically?
    Show answer

    Because the path changes, not the bandwidth. Before, data that didn't fit had to be staged or spilled over a much slower link (host memory over PCIe, or recomputation). Once everything fits, those transfers disappear. Capacity and bandwidth are separate dimensions, but capacity decides which bandwidth the data has to use.

Explain a memory cost from the physical route and the access pattern. Treat package boundaries and software-visible locality domains as related but separate facts.

Lesson 28

Multiple GPUs and nodes

LimitationOne accelerator's memory and compute run out, but splitting work across several means their partial results must be combined.
New mechanismCollective operations (all-reduce, all-gather, …) implemented by algorithms such as the ring, over direct GPU links and networks.
New stateRanks, communication buffers, and per-round schedules that all participants must agree on.
CostCommunication time that doesn't shrink as fast as compute, shared physical links, and exposed synchronization.

Partition the work, then account for its edges

More accelerators hold more data and do more work, but they must exchange values wherever their partitions interact. Strong scaling keeps the total work fixed and adds processors. Weak scaling grows the work with the processor count, keeping each one's share roughly fixed. A speedup number is incomplete until you say which. In data-parallel training, each worker processes different data and the gradient contributions are summed. Tensor partitioning splits an operation across devices and needs communication inside a layer. Pipeline partitioning gives each device some layers and passes activations between them. These can be combined, and none of them removes the serial or communication parts. A rank is one participant in a communication operation, a software identity (here, one per GPU), and all ranks must agree on each collective's order, size, type and operation.

Know what each collective promises

When may a rank use the result? Keep three events apart: the host has enqueued the collective, that rank's stream has completed it, and a consumer is allowed to use the result. In CUDA with NCCL, the call returning only means the operation was enqueued. A kernel launched later in the same stream is ordered after it automatically; a consumer in another stream needs an explicit dependency, such as an event. Completion is per rank and per stream: it doesn't require a global barrier, and ranks needn't finish at the same moment.

Try it: a ring all-reduce

Four ranks form a directed ring R0 → R1 → R2 → R3 → R0. Each starts with a four-element vector, split into four one-element chunks. Everyone wants the elementwise sum [1111, 2222, 3333, 4444]. In each of three reduce-scatter rounds, every rank sends one partial chunk to its neighbour, which adds it to its own. After those rounds each rank owns one fully summed chunk. Then three all-gather rounds pass the finished chunks around until everyone has all four. Every send in a round uses the state from before the round.

Derive the communication cost

With P ranks and an M-byte contribution each, split into P chunks: every rank sends M/P bytes in each of the P − 1 reduce-scatter rounds and the P − 1 all-gather rounds.

bytes sent per rank = 2 × (P − 1)/P × M
T_ring ≈ 2 × (P − 1) × α + 2 × (P − 1)/P × M / β

α is a fixed cost per round and β the bandwidth of each directed ring link, assuming identical full-duplex links (so sending and receiving happen at once; don't add the receive time again), equal chunks, no other traffic, and negligible time for the additions. With P = 4, M = 100 MB, β = 25 GB/s and α = 5 μs, each rank sends 150 MB: 6 ms of transfer plus 6 × 5 μs, so 6.03 ms.

Now add ranks. The bytes each rank sends, 2(P−1)/P × M, rise toward 2M, so the bandwidth term rises toward 2M/β from below; it never shrinks. Meanwhile the startup term 2(P−1)α grows linearly with P. Total communication time therefore doesn't level off, and it keeps increasing:

RanksBandwidth termStartup termAll-reduce time
46.00 ms0.03 ms6.03 ms
87.00 ms0.07 ms7.07 ms
167.50 ms0.15 ms7.65 ms

These come from the ideal model; measured collectives are usually slower, because links are shared, chunks are pipelined differently and software adds overhead.

A logical ring still runs on physical links

A direct GPU-to-GPU link, a PCIe path through a switch, a CPU interconnect and a network hop have very different bandwidth, latency and contention. Several logical ring edges may share one physical bottleneck: if every node pushes data through one network card or one oversubscribed switch port, adding logical connections doesn't add capacity at that cut. When reading bandwidth figures, check per-link versus aggregate, one direction versus both summed, raw line rate versus payload, and in-node links versus network. A "direct" device-to-device path avoids staging through CPU memory, but still needs mappings, permissions, synchronization and transfer time. It isn't free, and it isn't a coherence guarantee.

Overlap follows the dependency graph

The scaling model puts the all-reduce after all the compute. Real training frameworks split gradients into buckets and start reducing an early bucket while later ones are still being computed. Only ready data can be sent, and whoever uses a collective's result must wait for it to complete, so the last bucket's communication often stays exposed, and communication can steal memory bandwidth from compute. max(compute, communication) is an optimistic bound for perfect overlap; their sum describes full serialization. Neither proves what a program achieves: inspect a timeline, include the final synchronization, measure a whole iteration, and check accuracy when the reduction order changes.

PredictCheck with the ring and scaling explorers.
  1. After the three reduce-scatter rounds, does every rank have the complete result? Which complete chunk does each own?
    Show answer

    No, each has just one complete chunk: R0 has chunk 1 = 2222, R1 chunk 2 = 3333, R2 chunk 3 = 4444, R3 chunk 0 = 1111. The all-gather rounds distribute them.

  2. Four ranks, 100 MB each: how many bytes does each rank send across both phases? What's the modelled time at β = 25 GB/s, α = 5 μs?
    Show answer

    2 × 3/4 × 100 MB = 150 MB, taking 6 ms, plus 6 rounds × 5 μs = 0.03 ms: 6.03 ms. Each rank also receives 150 MB, at the same time over the full-duplex links, so that isn't added again.

  3. With 40 ms of total compute, what are the runtime, speedup and efficiency at four ranks in the blocking model? And at eight?
    Show answer

    P = 4: 10 + 6.03 = 16.03 ms, speedup ≈ 2.50×, efficiency ≈ 62%. P = 8: 5 + 7 + 0.07 = 12.07 ms, ≈ 3.31×, ≈ 41%. Compute divides perfectly here; communication doesn't.

  4. Two logical ring edges share one 25 GB/s physical link. Why is the model optimistic? Does a "direct" path remove the need to wait for completion?
    Show answer

    The model gave each edge its own 25 GB/s; two edges sharing a link split it, so transfers slow or queue. A direct path skips a CPU buffer but still takes time, and anyone consuming the data or reusing the buffer must still wait for completion.

  5. A collective needs a gradient bucket that hasn't been computed yet. Can launching it asynchronously overlap that bucket's production with its transfer? How can earlier buckets still overlap?
    Show answer

    No: data can't be sent before it exists; an async launch just queues behind the dependency. But buckets that are already finished can be reduced while later buckets are computed, subject to contention. The last bucket's communication often remains on the critical path.

  6. Transfer. In the 4-rank ring, the edges R0 → R1 and R2 → R3 share one physical 25 GB/s link. For M = 100 MB, what lower bound does that shared link put on the all-reduce's transfer time?
    Show answer

    Each ring edge carries 2 × 3/4 × 100 MB = 150 MB. Two edges on one link means 300 MB must cross it, so the transfer takes at least 300 MB ÷ 25 GB/s = 12 ms, twice the ideal 6 ms, before adding the α terms. Mark the shared link on the diagram first: the bound comes from total bytes over the scarcest shared resource.

Before quoting a bandwidth or claiming a scaling result, draw the collective's data dependencies and the physical routes it uses.

Unit 6 · Summary

Putting it together

Trace one hypothetical training iteration. A CPU prepares input in host memory. Four GPUs each receive a chunk, run low-precision matrix operations with a specified accumulator, combine 4-byte gradients with an all-reduce, then return a compact result to the CPU.

  1. Name the representations. Input elements, scale metadata, accumulators, communication buffers, output conversion. Quantizing the inputs doesn't shrink an FP32 gradient buffer.
  2. Name the physical homes. Which CPU node holds the host data, which GPU owns each device allocation. A shared pointer name isn't a placement.
  3. Draw every movement. Host → GPU, HBM → on-chip tiles, GPU → neighbour GPU, GPU → host. One byte can cross several boundaries, each with its own count.
  4. Establish the handoffs. Input produced before transfer; transfer before use; local gradients before the reduction; reduction complete before its result is used; output transfer complete before the CPU reads it.
  5. Find the critical path. Overlap independent chunks only where engines and dependencies allow, and include initialization, conversion, launches, transfers, collectives and synchronization in the interval you optimize.
  6. Validate. Compare with a numerical reference, then measure steady-state and first-use behaviour separately. A faster wrong answer doesn't count.
One segment, worked out

With 10 ms of local compute per GPU and a 100 MB gradient all-reduced over the lesson's 25 GB/s ring, the compute-then-communicate segment takes 10 + 6.03 = 16.03 ms. That's not the whole iteration: host transfers, conversions and the CPU's consumption of the output are extra stages. To improve it, find the largest exposed stage and test one hypothesis: fewer bytes, better locality, cheaper conversion, faster useful compute, or legal overlap. Check numerical quality after every precision or reduction-order change.

Transfer exercise: annotate a path and a time budget

A fresh hypothetical setup, with its own numbers. One CPU node holds a 400 MB input batch in host memory. Four GPUs each copy their 100 MB share over their own 25 GB/s host link (assume the links and host memory don't contend). Each GPU computes for 12 ms and produces a 200 MB FP32 gradient, which is all-reduced over a ring with β = 50 GB/s per link and α = 10 μs. Each GPU then returns a 4 MB result to the host over its 25 GB/s link, and the CPU reads it. Assume the result is proportional to the local input (16 MB in total for the batch) and that local compute time scales in proportion to the local batch. Assume every stage waits for the previous one: no overlap.

  1. Draw where the input, the gradients and the outputs live, and mark every copy and every completion dependency.
  2. Compute each stage's time and the critical-path estimate for one iteration.
  3. Name the bottleneck, and propose one measurement that could disprove your prediction.
  4. Now use 8 GPUs. Compare strong scaling (same 400 MB batch) with weak scaling (each GPU keeps 100 MB, so the batch doubles). The gradient stays 200 MB because it depends on the model, not the batch.
Show a worked answer

Path. Host memory on the CPU's node → (copy, then completion) → each GPU's HBM → compute → gradient in HBM → ring all-reduce over GPU links (each rank's consumer waits for that rank's all-reduce to complete, not for a global barrier) → 4 MB result copied to host memory → the CPU may read only after that copy completes.

Stages. Copy in: 100 MB ÷ 25 GB/s = 4 ms. Compute: 12 ms. All-reduce: 2 × 3/4 × 200 MB ÷ 50 GB/s = 6 ms, plus 6 × 10 μs = 0.06 ms, so 6.06 ms. Copy out: 4 MB ÷ 25 GB/s = 0.16 ms. Critical path: 4 + 12 + 6.06 + 0.16 = 22.22 ms.

Bottleneck. Compute is the largest stage (54%), then the all-reduce (27%). The obvious improvement is overlapping gradient buckets' communication with the rest of the backward pass, which could hide much of the 6.06 ms; the final ready bucket leaves a communication tail that can't be hidden behind earlier backward-pass computation in this schedule, though other independent work or a different schedule could change how much stays exposed.

A disproving measurement. Profile one iteration's timeline (for example with Nsight Systems) and time the all-reduce by itself at 200 MB. If it takes much longer than 6.06 ms, the ring's links are shared or slower than assumed, and the "compute is the bottleneck" conclusion may be wrong too.

8 GPUs, strong scaling. Copy in 50 MB = 2 ms; compute 6 ms (assuming it halves with the batch); all-reduce 2 × 7/8 × 200 MB ÷ 50 GB/s = 7 ms + 14 × 10 μs, so 7.14 ms; copy out 2 MB = 0.08 ms (the 16 MB result, split eight ways). (If instead each GPU still returned a fixed 4 MB, copy-out would stay 0.16 ms and the total would be 15.30 ms, about 1.45×.) Total 15.22 ms: a speedup of 22.22 / 15.22 ≈ 1.46× on twice the GPUs, because the all-reduce grew while everything else halved.

8 GPUs, weak scaling. Copy in 4 ms, compute 12 ms, all-reduce 7.14 ms, copy out 0.16 ms: 23.30 ms for an 800 MB batch. Throughput goes from 400/22.22 ≈ 18.0 to 800/23.30 ≈ 34.3 MB of input per ms, about 1.91×. Weak scaling looks better because each GPU's compute share stays large compared with the communication.
